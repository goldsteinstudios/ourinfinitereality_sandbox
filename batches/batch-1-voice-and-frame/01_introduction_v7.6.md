# Our Infinite Reality

*An introduction.*

I'm a stay-at-home dad and a woodworker. I have no academic credentials in any of the fields this project touches. A few years ago I asked myself a question about infinity that I couldn't put down, and I've been following where it led ever since. This is the workshop where that following happens.

Here's the question, and I'd ask you to actually sit with it for a second rather than skip past it:

**What if reality has no smallest piece and no largest whole — no floor and no ceiling — all the way down and all the way out?**

Not as a mystical claim. As a plain structural assumption. *If* that's true — if you can always divide further and always extend further, if there's no final grain and no final edge — then some things have to follow. Not because I say so. Because of what the assumption itself forces.

---

## What follows, in plain words

If there's no smallest piece, then no point ever fully resolves. Every "thing" you try to pin down turns out to be made of relationships, and those relationships are made of finer relationships, and it never bottoms out into a solid dot you can stand on. So a strange consequence appears: to be anything at all, a thing has to be *distinguishable* from what it isn't. Pure sameness — total indistinction, nothing to tell apart — can't exist, because the moment you'd point at it, you've distinguished it, and it's gone.

That impossible pure-sameness is the seed of the whole thing. It can't be. And *because* it can't be, something has to. That's the first move — not "something from nothing," but "something, *because* nothing won't hold still." The empty center that can't be occupied is exactly what makes everything turn.

From there the pattern keeps repeating. Every stable structure I've been able to look at closely turns out to be built the same way: it organizes *around* a center it can never actually occupy. A wheel works because the hub is empty. A whirlpool is a shape made of water rushing around a middle that stays hollow. A hurricane has its eye. An atom is mostly the space its electrons orbit. A galaxy turns around a center. Your own sense of "now" is like this — you can never quite land on it; the moment you grab it, it's already behind you, and you keep circling it your whole life.

**Stable things don't sit on solid foundations. They rotate around empty centers.** That's the whole recognition, in one sentence. Everything else is me trying to check whether it's really true, in as many different places as I can find.

---

## Where I've been checking

I test the pattern in three places that had no business agreeing with each other, and I keep the three tests strictly separate so they can't contaminate one another.

**The math.** I try to derive the pattern from that single starting assumption — no floor, no ceiling — using nothing but structural logic, seeing how far it goes on its own before I bring anything else in. This is the spine. If the math doesn't hold, nothing else matters, and I've thrown out plenty of it when it didn't. (The most honest moments in this whole project are the ones where the math flatly refused to give me what I wanted, and I had to change the model instead of the math.)

**The physics.** I look at whether working physics — real conservation laws, quantum uncertainty, the geometry of spacetime — shows the same shape. Sometimes it does, cleanly enough to startle me. Sometimes it only sort of does, and I mark those as maybes rather than pretending they're more.

**The ancient text.** This is the strangest part, and I understand if it raises an eyebrow. There's a 2,400-year-old Chinese text, the Dao De Jing, whose oldest surviving version was dug out of a tomb on bamboo strips. When I read it not as poetry or philosophy but as a compressed technical description — a set of field notes — it appears to be describing the exact same structure: the empty center, the turning around it, the way one frame gives birth to the next. I don't read the characters for their dictionary meanings. I read them for the shapes and operations built into them. The convergence is uncanny often enough that I keep going, and disciplined enough that I flag every place it might just be me seeing faces in clouds.

The point of three separate tests is simple. No single instrument sees the whole of anything. A CT scan and an MRI show you different shadows of the same body. If three completely different descriptions — a piece of math, a body of physics, an ancient text — keep landing on the same structure, that convergence is worth something. Not proof. Something.

---

## What this is not

**Not a religion.** No beliefs required, no guru, nothing to join. If anything, the project runs the other way: it suggests that some things we've filed under "spiritual" might be technical observations that got mystified as they were passed down and the original meaning was lost.

**Not a Theory of Everything.** I'm not claiming to have solved reality. I'm pointing at a pattern and asking whether you see it too.

**Not finished, and not certain.** This is pattern recognition, not proof. I could be wrong. The whole archive is versioned and public, wrong turns included, precisely so you can watch the thinking correct itself instead of having to trust a polished result. When the model breaks, I'd rather you see the break and the repair than a smooth surface hiding both.

## How I actually work

I bring the questions and the pattern-hunches. I use AI as a collaborator to build tools, to articulate what I'm half-seeing, and — most important — to try to knock the ideas down. The best thing a collaborator does here isn't agree with me; it's find the place where the pattern *doesn't* hold and make me look at it. A lot of this project is that: proposing something, testing it hard, and keeping careful track of what survived the testing versus what only sounded good.

There's a principle I hold onto, borrowed from the way the pattern itself behaves. I call it *constant accuracy, variable precision*: a good observation can point reliably in the right direction even when it can't yet say exactly how far. Being roughly right about the shape of something is worth more than being precisely wrong about its details. So I try to keep the direction honest and let the precision catch up over time.

And one more, which is really the heart of it. The thing I'm reaching for can't be fully captured — that's not a flaw in the reaching, it's the nature of what's being reached for. The center recedes when you grab at it. The truth exceeds any single way of saying it. That's not cause for despair; it's the condition for everything worth doing. You draw the diagram knowing the diagram isn't the thing. You say it anyway, knowing the saying falls short. The gap between what is and what can be said isn't the obstacle. It's the medium you work in.

---

## Where to go from here

If you're skeptical — good, you should be — start with what would prove me wrong. I keep an explicit list of what would falsify this, and I'd rather you attack it there than take anything on faith.

If you want the pattern itself, look at the wheel and its empty hub, then at a whirlpool, then at an atom, and ask whether it's the same shape.

If you want the machinery, the math is all there, versioned, breaks and repairs included.

And if you want the ancient field guide, the text is being worked through character by character, with the oldest bamboo-strip evidence marked wherever it exists.

Enter anywhere. That's the one thing the pattern promises: because every stable structure is built around a center that's everywhere and nowhere, there's no wrong door.
