## Tolerance

I'm reminded of the example that Alan Watts gives in one of his books, of the tolerance level within a system and how the thermostat works in an air conditioner, and that it's not just about how warm or how cold you set the setting but how much tolerance you leave before the machine kicks in, and then again before it kicks off. So if you're heating a house and it's 60° and you set the thing to 65°, will it heat up to 65° and stop? And then wait until it gets back down to 60°? It's the buffer. So if you set it to 68 the thing will heat the house up to 72, then wait until it gets down to 66, and then it'll heat it back up to 72. And the closer you get to it being perfect the more often the machine has to click on and off, and the clicking on and off is hard on the machine, and so the machine doesn't last as long. And if it becomes too close, if you're too in system, if it has to be 68.005°, then the thing is not going to work, because it's going to flip back and forth and short circuit.

Look at the sailor and the waves, and the fact that she decides she can get used to a certain amount of discomfort instead of forcing herself to find that perfect balance that would give her no discomfort. The idea is the amount of discomfort she gets from the waves, relative to the amount of discomfort she gets from the effort to remove that discomfort. You shift that then to the discomfort versus how much effort she wants to put in. And if she's willing to accept a certain level of discomfort, and maybe she can become more tolerant over time, the less she uses her body the more it will be resilient. She'll start to build up calluses and muscles in the right places over time, but only if she's not completely overwhelmed with effort all of the time. Does that make sense?

I think she still tries to find the perfect balance point to avoid discomfort, exhausting herself in the process. Doesn't that seem important?

## Pyramids and mounds

Consider the cascade that falls out of the pivot from thinking reality is infinite to thinking it is finite. Knowable. Ownable. Dominable. Loseable.

It creates surplus, which creates hierarchy, which creates power, which creates extraction and destruction and domination.

Compare pyramid builders to mound builders. One is a linear creation with a finite solution requiring great force and effort. The other is done when the conditions are right, and quietly abandoned when it no longer has meaning to the people building it.

Even Mesoamerican pyramids seem to be built as layers atop previous smaller pyramids, rather than one course at a time. Think of the difference. In course by course you need great labor and resources and simple technology at the start, and then by the end you need specialized labor, specific resources, and complex technology. So the first stone laid in the Khufu pyramid assumed all of that could be done. Linear thinking. The Mesoamericans would only build when a burst of prosperity allowed it. Like a bud beginning to form on a node.

Mounds and henges are celebrations of societies remaining a going concern, to borrow an economics term. 天地不仁.

## The text

Why do I refer to the text as 老子 and not the 道德經 written by a person referred to as 老子?

My running theory about how the 老子 came to be: humans considering reality over tens of thousands of years, and Chu script being a technological breakthrough that allowed it to be written as a shorthand for a semi-literate culture that was perhaps trying to find a new way to organize humans after the violence of the Warring States era. Or something to that effect.

It might be that you have to envision a different mechanism for storing and transporting and communicating this information. So if you existed in a semi-literate society, and had certain people who knew this and carried around these tubes filled with rolls of bamboo strips with these little shorthand things in them, you might be teaching, sitting down in the dirt with somebody and saying, okay, and then you draw a loop in and of itself and you say, we're going to call this 玄. This is going to be our infinite mysterious paradoxical thread. And then we're going to watch this. And then you cut it in half. And together, what is a lesson about cutting an infinite thread turns into the character 絕, which means to sever. It's that the transmission mechanism changed. And so now people are reading a text that is being copied widely, and they're reading it themselves without the person standing there drawing the glyphs for them. And so it becomes a different way of storing and transporting and transmitting and reproducing. When you have a hardened language, more people can access it. If you have to know a special shorthand, and you have to know how to weave all the lessons together, and you have to know how to draw all the characters in order to make sense of the lesson, that's more sophisticated than to just have to read a text one character at a time.

And so when you lose that nuance by hardening those characters, you have to fill in the blanks of what you lost in the understanding. And so that's why I think the received text has so many chapters that are missing, quote unquote, in the Guodian Laozi. It's not that they were destroyed or they were not finished. It's that the concepts are already fully fleshed out if you read the shorthand as shorthand and understand how the lessons work. But that doesn't make sense, or you miss some of the important nuance, when you just read each character as a word. And so then you have to backfill, and they did a phenomenal job, obviously. Chapter one of the received text is a master class. But if the language hadn't been hardened, it may not have been necessary.

I don't think the history or paleography specifically points to scribal error. Look into the Han and Qin consolidations and the idea that language was systematized and hardened, so that where a Chu glyph may have been a shorthand meant to open a lesson's worth of meaning, that nuance of depth of meaning disappeared.

Obviously this is a theory that I have no way of proving, but given the rest of the work I've done, it would make a lot of sense. And that's my stance.

The Guodian has both 恆 and 常, used differently.

Bottom line, humans have been anatomically equivalent for almost 300,000 years, and we have to assume that means their intellectual ability has also been equivalent. Not linearly progressive, but locally adaptive. Until about 10,000 years ago anyway.

## The boat

I think the boat is the constructs that we live in. It's the finite slice of reality that we experience and the structure that drives our experience. It's something that you can't escape without jumping into the water, because in addition to just rowing, the constructs are keeping you afloat as well.

The reality of the human moment we are in now is that it's the culmination of the hubris that began with the advent of agriculture, at the point where humans no longer said we are at the whim of the chaos of the universe, but we are now in control, because we want to manipulate and engineer the food ecosystem to allow us to grow beyond the natural carrying capacity of our environment. And so that construct is the boat that we live in. It has other things in it that change in shape, but the basic fundamental boat that we live in is that humans are separate from the rest of the ecosystem, that they are dominant, and that they are therefore not just allowed to, but have a divine mandate to engineer the environment to meet their own needs. And that's where we are, and it is what it is.

I wonder if one of the dynamics is that when her people invented boats that could go out onto the ocean, they gave up their sense of needing to know how to swim in the ocean. And so she literally never learned how to swim in the ocean. And so if she were to fall out of this boat, she would be swept under immediately. She never learned to swim, not because they said, oh, you're not going to swim, but because they just didn't learn how to swim in that society. And they hadn't since the days of shipbuilding.
